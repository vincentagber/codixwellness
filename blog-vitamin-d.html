Vitamin D in the UK: What You Should Know During Autumn and Winter

As the days become shorter and sunlight becomes less intense, maintaining adequate vitamin D levels can be more difficult. This is particularly important in the UK, where the body may not produce enough vitamin D from sunlight between October and early March.

Why Is Vitamin D Important?

Vitamin D helps the body absorb calcium and phosphorus, two minerals needed to maintain healthy bones, teeth and muscles. It also contributes to the normal function of the immune system.

Although vitamin D can be obtained from certain foods, sunlight is one of its main natural sources. The body produces vitamin D when the skin is exposed to direct sunlight.

Why Is Vitamin D Important During Autumn and Winter?

During autumn and winter, sunlight in the UK does not contain enough ultraviolet B radiation for the skin to produce sufficient vitamin D. This means people must rely more heavily on food and supplements during this period.

UK government guidance recommends that adults and children over four years old consider taking a daily supplement containing 10 micrograms (400 IU) of vitamin D between October and early March.

Who May Be More Prone to Low Vitamin D Levels?

Some people may be more likely to have insufficient vitamin D and may need to consider taking a daily supplement throughout the year. This includes people who:

  • Spend most of their time indoors
  • Live in a care home or are housebound
  • Usually cover most of their skin when outdoors
  • Have darker skin, including people of African, African-Caribbean or South Asian heritage

Higher levels of melanin in darker skin can reduce the amount of vitamin D the body produces from sunlight. People within these groups should speak with a pharmacist, GP or other qualified healthcare professional if they are unsure about their vitamin D needs.

Which Foods Contain Vitamin D?

Only a limited number of foods naturally contain significant amounts of vitamin D. Sources include:

  • Oily fish, such as salmon, sardines, trout, herring and mackerel
  • Egg yolks
  • Red meat
  • Liver (although liver should be avoided during pregnancy)
  • Fortified breakfast cereals and fat spreads
  • Foods specifically fortified with vitamin D

In the UK, ordinary cow's milk is generally not fortified with vitamin D, so it should not be relied upon as a major source. A varied and balanced diet remains important, but it can be difficult to obtain enough vitamin D from food alone during autumn and winter.

Choosing a Vitamin D Supplement

When selecting a supplement, read the label carefully and check the amount of vitamin D provided in each daily serving. For most adults, the recommended daily amount during autumn and winter is 10 micrograms (400 IU).

Avoid combining several supplements containing vitamin D without checking the total amount. The NHS advises adults and children aged 11 years and over not to take more than 100 micrograms (4,000 IU) daily, unless instructed by a healthcare professional, as excessive intake over time may be harmful.

People with certain health conditions, those taking medication, pregnant or breastfeeding women, and anyone who has been diagnosed with a vitamin D deficiency should seek individual guidance from a healthcare professional.

Make Vitamin D Part of Your Winter Wellness Routine

Supporting your vitamin D intake is a simple but important part of maintaining your wellbeing during the darker months. Alongside a balanced diet and an active lifestyle, taking an appropriate daily vitamin D supplement can help support normal bones, muscles and immune function.

As autumn approaches, check your routine and consider whether you are getting enough vitamin D. If you are uncertain, speak to your pharmacist or GP for personalised advice.
